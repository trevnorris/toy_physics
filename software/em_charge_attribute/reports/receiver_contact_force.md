# How much normal force does the receiver transmit back to the slab?

**One receiver gives a definite answer.** Take a single radius-one opening into an unbounded four-dimensional half-space. The receiver is stationary, incompressible, irrotational and inviscid, with unit mass and number densities and zero far velocity. Use the feeding solver's isolated continuum profile \(j(r)\propto(1-r^2)^3\) inside the opening and zero outside, normalized to intake \(1/4\). The plane is fixed and \(+w\) points into the receiver. These are benchmark assumptions. [Profile and feeding boundary](../../stage1_solver/tools/lateral_reservoir_field.py) · [Half-space kernel](../../stage1_solver/reports/one_mouth_outer_holding_force.md)

Bernoulli gives the excess receiver pressure \(\Delta p=-\rho(|v_\parallel|^2+v_w^2)/2\). On the intact plane \(v_w=0\), so the reduction in pressure produces an excess force toward \(+w\). Integration over the intact plane gives **+0.00179847622402** in solver units. A separate Mathematica calculation inside the aperture gives carriage \(315/(2288\pi)\), integrated excess pressure \(-0.04202478170775244\), and their sum **+0.001798476224019436**. The two calculations use disjoint surfaces. All the excess normal momentum entering this particular receiver is returned through pressure on the intact plane. The far velocity decreases as inverse distance cubed, so the excess stress integrated over a distant hemisphere vanishes. A finite mass discharge is compatible with zero net normal momentum export at infinity. [Guarded calculations, scripts and inputs](../../../docs/_measurements/receiver_contact_force.json)

**Can this receiver be attached to the saved feeding mouth?** For each of the three fixed-slab fields, drive the half-space with that field's own cellwise number current. Compare the central phase to the mean at two opposite points half a mouth radius away on the same diameter. This contrast removes a common phase offset and any uniform in-plane phase gradient. On the finer grid it is **+0.0501744127449 radians** in the feeding field and **−0.0499484938296 radians** in the receiver. The signs also oppose on both coarser grids. The receiving phase uses the Neumann kernel integrated over each cylindrical cell. The feed uses an even radial extrapolation to the axis, with the first-ring contrast as a sensitivity check. A positive change of constant receiver density rescales the ideal contrast and preserves its sign. [Same-current readouts](../../../docs/_measurements/receiver_contact_force.json)

**What the physical contact must supply.** The conservative, single-trace surface energy \(g(n,\chi,R)\) allows a jump in the normal carrier derivative while requiring continuous phase. Altering its coefficient cannot join these two fixed traces. At a stationary flat patch with conserved number flux density \(j\), the carrier delivers \(T_{\rm feed}-T_{\rm recv}+m j^2(1/n_{\rm feed}-1/n_{\rm recv})\) per area to the intervening layer. \(T\) includes pressure and density-gradient stress. The force delivered onward to the slab also depends on the layer's support, edge stresses, and any momentum stored there. Direct receiver pressure on the exterior intact plane is a separate contact. Adding the benchmark pressure to the archived feed forces would mix incompatible solutions. The missing datum is a joint mouth boundary relation for the two material states and the normal traction delivered to the slab. A coupled solution may change both phase profiles. An effective layer retaining these particular traces would have to support their spatially varying phase difference and account independently for its normal force.
