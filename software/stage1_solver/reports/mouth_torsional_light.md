# Can opposite turns of the two faces carry a wave and a mouth's signed flux?

**Yes in a specified mechanical medium.** Four angular springs around each elementary square transmit a transverse wave. If the springs register each material face separately, that same medium can preserve one signed turn around a prepared mouth all the way across an ordered exterior. A small tilt of one mouth patch gives a torque pattern that reverses on its mirror and couples to a traveling wave. The test keeps the mouth center fixed. Identifying this motion with physical light and predicting forces on free mouths are open. ## Which material moves?

Use a three-dimensional cubic array with lattice spacing one. Each directed edge supports two material angles that counterrotate, with registrations \((e^{ia_e},e^{-ia_e})\) in a common frame. Each square has four angular sliders of its own. For an oriented edge of that square let \(x_j=\phi_{j+1}-\phi_j-\epsilon_j a_e\), where \(\epsilon_j\) is the direction of traversal. An individual contact costs \(K(1-\cos x_j)\). The conjugate face carries the opposite angles and a copy of the same energy. Angles and their positive inertias belong to identifiable material elements. Exact counterrotation, separate registrations, and freely rolling corner contacts are constitutive assumptions. The frame is held in the wave calculation. Turning a torque through a right-angle bearing can load its housing. The generalized angular balance alone leaves the recoil of a free slab undetermined. Relative angles measured against co-rotating material references survive a rigid rotation of the apparatus. [Static controls](../tools/mouth_torsion_flux.py)

**Power.** In the linear regime, static torque balance shares the circulation \(F=\sum_{e\in\partial p}\epsilon_e a_e\) equally among four springs. Their energy per face is \(KF^2/8\). A long transverse wave moves the edges and the corners, giving an effective inertia \(I+2J\) per direction, for edge and corner inertias \(I,J\) per face. The squared wave speed is \(K/[4(I+2J)]\) for both polarizations. Computing the actual torque times angular velocity across a cell gives the group velocity times the full mechanical wave energy. Separate Wolfram and Python calculations give this result on a lattice axis. The Python check also resolves two off-axis directions. For \(I=2,J=1,K=8\), at wave number 0.04 along a lattice axis the measured power divided by energy was 0.707018387, approaching \(1/\sqrt2\). Opening one spring on every square removes the static restoring torque. Pinning all the corners instead makes a uniform rotation cost energy. The mechanical frequency scale can equally be calculated at \(K=1\) by rescaling time. [Wolfram derivation](../tools/mouth_torsion_loop.wl) · [Three-dimensional power calculation](../tools/mouth_torsion_power.py)

**Other motions.** At nonzero wavevector there are also four physical motions with no restoring force. Three rotate all four sliders on an individual square together. The fourth changes edge angles by differences between neighboring vertex angles while moving the sliders to preserve spring extensions. The marked elements can store kinetic energy in these motions. In a periodic \(15^3\) box, an impulse on one edge deposited 0.186488802823 of its initial kinetic energy in that subspace. Four oriented impulses around a square gave a fraction of \(1.45\times10^{-30}\), consistent with exact cancellation. The closed drive can still excite higher-frequency modes. [Impulse calculation](../tools/mouth_torsion_probes.py)

## What keeps a signed unit visible outside?

A contact sensing only the relative angle of the faces reads \(e^{2ia}\). On a cube, two primitive face circulations arbitrarily close to \(\pi\) and four small ones can sum to \(2\pi\), yet the relative-angle contact sees nearly zero energy on every square. A distant primitive count cannot be inferred from that contact. The alternative tested here registers a full turn of each face separately. After all four sliders relax, its per-face energy is \(W(F)=4K[1-\cos(F/4)]\) for the principal circulation \(-\pi\le F\le\pi\). Equal spring torques and evaluation of the other stationary branches give this minimum. The cost at \(F=\pi\) is positive. A half-turn around a neutral material square distinguishes the laws before introducing a mouth. Small-angle torque versus rotation on separate elements would measure the spring stiffness. Their angular acceleration under a known torque would measure the two inertias. [Compact spring and branch tests](../tools/mouth_torsion_flux.py)

Use a closed cubic cut of integer half-width \(R\) sampling the radial ordering directions of the earlier round mouth. \(R\) is in lattice spacings. The inner cube carries the attachment energy and samples a prescribed radial mouth geometry. Outer enclosing cuts only measure the resulting flux. The outer boundary is free and the energy minimization ranges over every allowed link configuration. Sites have two-component internal states, as in the earlier mouth law. Their directions \(p_i\) prefer the local face normals \(n_i=\pm\hat r_i\). The surface pays \(S(1-p_i\cdot n_i)\) per site and \(G(|v|-\mathrm{Re}[e^{-ia}v])\) per bond, with neighboring overlap \(v=z_i^\dagger z_j\). Separate site-tilt and bond-slip work measurements on an ordered patch could fix these two strengths. A common material registration allows conjugate face reference states \(z_b=i\sigma_y z_t^*\) in the radial trial. The two opposite normals by themselves determine only the loop curvatures. The origin of a physically consistent registration across sites remains a requirement. Three bounds compare *all* configurations on a finite ordered annulus, for any outer cut farther than the mouth. All costs below include both faces unless stated otherwise.

* In the aligned trial use the overlap phases as edge angles throughout the annulus. Each principal circulation is half the solid angle of its square as viewed from the center, with sign reversed for the mirror. It sums to one signed turn on each enclosing cut. For a square whose center has integer part \(m\) of its maximum absolute coordinate, \(|F|\le1/(2m^2)\). There are \(72m^2+48m+12\) squares in the entire shell. Summing \(W(F)\le KF^2/8\) to infinity gives a two-face trial upper bound \(K(72+48/R+12/R^2)/[16(R-1)]\).
* To change the count between two cuts requires a charged elementary cube or a principal-angle tie. Convexity of \(W\) and a total circulation of at least \(2\pi\) on the cube cost at least \(12W(\pi/3)=1.635560338125K\). A tie on one square costs \(2W(\pi)=2.343145750508K\). Each comparison counts each physical square once.
* At \(S=400K,G=4K\), tilting even one site by 0.1 radians costs at least \(1.998333888790K\). For smaller tilts set \(u=4\sin(0.1/4)\), \(h=\cos[1/(2R)]\). In a shortest-phase comparison with the radial state, the complex overlap changes by at most \(u\), its modulus stays above \(h-u\), and its argument changes by at most \(\arcsin(u/h)\). A change of the mouth count requires one of the four links on some square to cover the distance to a principal-angle cut during interpolation. On each face some link must then miss its local preferred phase by at least \(b=(\pi-1/(2R^2))/4-\arcsin(u/h)\). Exact counterrotation makes both primitive counts wrong together. The resulting two-bond cost is at least \(2G(h-u)(1-\cos b)\).

## Can a prepared mouth couple to the wave?

At the middle of the positive-x sampling face, tilt one radial site toward the positive-y direction by a small angle and give the mirror the reflected tilt. Hold the edges at their prepared, locally aligned values. For one face the linear incremental torque is \(\tau=G|v|(\delta\arg v+B\chi)\), where \(B\chi\) is the difference between the added rolls of neighboring sites. The reference preparation transports the tilted site by the shortest rotation. If sites can physically roll about their own normals and settle, minimization gives \(B^T\tau=0\). Thus the torque closes into loops and cannot drive the longitudinal zero mode. Fixing the roll produces a nonzero divergence. The energy model must specify whether this roll is a material motion available on the forcing timescale. Changing the phase convention alone cannot perform that experiment. [Contact calculation](../tools/mouth_torsion_contact.py)

At \(R=5\) the relaxed, single-face torque norm per radian was 0.222422219 for \(G=4\). Mirroring reversed every torque, and the flat control gave zero. The net coupling to a spatially uniform edge rotation is zero. Projecting the same torque onto the two mass-normalized unperturbed traveling modes gives nonzero reciprocal virtual work at finite wavelength. Along a lattice axis the norm divided by \(|k|\) was 0.504225082 at \(kR=0.05\), in agreement with the long-wave torque moment prediction 0.504410242. With doubled cut half-width the respective values were 0.509339456 and 0.509526655. These are single-face amplitudes. At fixed primitive wave amplitude the conjugate face doubles the generalized torque. The normalized wave overlap uses \(I=2,J=1\); uniform rescaling of all spring strengths leaves the eigenvectors unchanged. This tests a prepared contact and an imposed wave. A moving mouth, the emitted power, and the self-consistent response of the exterior were not measured. [Wave overlap and moment control](../tools/mouth_torsion_wave_overlap.py)

**What would discriminate the candidate next?** Compare a probe coupled to the model's existing optical material displacement with one coupled to the counterrotating elements, on the same uncharged piece of brane. Exact face symmetry still forbids linear transfer from ordinary in-phase displacement to the signed wave. If the established light readout sees only the in-phase displacement, this candidate predicts an additional wave species and cannot identify the two. A physical proposal for the optical readout must fix the coupling, match both polarizations, and account for recoil and the loss of shear order toward the bulk. The older optical calculation explicitly supplies a transverse material displacement and its shear modulus divided by density. It leaves its microscopic origin open. Trace the co-moving and opposite constituent displacements seen by a physical material probe, then determine whether the same contacts lose shear stiffness as that material enters the disordered bulk. The signed count and the optical frequency leave electric force calibration and 0PN free fall open.
